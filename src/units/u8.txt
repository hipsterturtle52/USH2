## The big picture
This is the **most heavily weighted unit**, and it carries a large share of the social and cultural questions. Between 1954 and 1975 four things happened at once:
- The **civil rights movement** destroyed legal segregation and won federal protection of voting rights.
- **Lyndon Johnson's Great Society** built the largest expansion of federal social programs since the New Deal.
- The **Warren Court** expanded individual rights.
- Women, Latinos, Native Americans, gay Americans, and environmentalists launched their own movements.

Over all of it hung the **Vietnam War**. It split the country, drove LBJ from office, fed a youth **counterculture**, and ended in American withdrawal.

## 1. The civil rights movement, phase one (1954–1960)
**Brown v. Board of Education** (1954) was the legal breakthrough. NAACP lawyer **Thurgood Marshall** argued it, and a unanimous Court under Chief Justice **Earl Warren** ruled that **segregated public schools are "inherently unequal."** That overturned *Plessy* in education. A second ruling (1955) ordered desegregation "with all deliberate speed," which southern officials treated as permission to delay. Over 100 southern congressmen signed the **Southern Manifesto** (1956) pledging **"massive resistance."**

Key events of the 1950s:
- **Emmett Till** (1955), a 14-year-old from Chicago, was murdered in Mississippi. His mother's open-casket funeral shocked the nation.
- **Montgomery Bus Boycott** (1955–56): after **Rosa Parks** refused to give up her bus seat, Black residents boycotted the buses for **381 days**, led by a young minister, **Martin Luther King Jr.** A Supreme Court ruling ended bus segregation. King and others then formed the **Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC)** in 1957, committed to **nonviolent** direct action.
- **Little Rock** (1957): Arkansas governor **Orval Faubus** used the National Guard to block nine Black students from Central High School. **Eisenhower** sent the **101st Airborne** to enforce the court order.
- The **Civil Rights Act of 1957** was the first since Reconstruction. It was weak, but it created a Civil Rights Commission.

## 2. Phase two: direct action (1960–1965)
- **Sit-ins** (1960): four Black college students sat at a whites-only **Woolworth's lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina**. Sit-ins spread across the South. With help from **Ella Baker**, students formed the **Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC)**.
- **Freedom Rides** (1961): **CORE** sent integrated groups on interstate buses to test desegregation rulings. Mobs firebombed a bus in Alabama, and the Kennedy administration finally sent federal marshals.
- **James Meredith** integrated the University of Mississippi (1962) under federal protection.
- **Birmingham** (1963): police chief **Bull Connor** turned **fire hoses and police dogs** on marching children, and the images went around the world. Jailed, King wrote his **"Letter from Birmingham Jail,"** defending civil disobedience against unjust laws.
- **March on Washington** (August 1963): about 250,000 people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial, where King gave his **"I Have a Dream"** speech. Weeks later, a bomb at Birmingham's **16th Street Baptist Church** killed four girls.

After Kennedy's assassination, **Lyndon Johnson** used his legislative skill to break a southern filibuster and pass the **Civil Rights Act of 1964**:
- It banned segregation in **public accommodations** (hotels, restaurants, theaters).
- Its **Title VII** banned employment discrimination by race, color, religion, national origin, **and sex**.
- It created the **Equal Employment Opportunity Commission** (EEOC).

The **Twenty-fourth Amendment** (1964) outlawed **poll taxes** in federal elections.

**Voting rights:** In **Freedom Summer** (1964), volunteers registered Black voters in Mississippi, and three civil rights workers were murdered. **Fannie Lou Hamer's** **Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party** challenged the all-white state delegation at the Democratic convention. In **Selma** (March 1965), state troopers beat marchers, including **John Lewis**, on **"Bloody Sunday."** Days later Johnson called for the **Voting Rights Act of 1965**. It suspended **literacy tests**, sent **federal examiners** to register voters, and required federal **preclearance** of voting changes in covered states. Black registration in the South soared.

## 3. Phase three: Black Power and urban unrest (1965–1968)
Legal victories did not end northern poverty, police abuse, or housing segregation. Riots broke out in **Watts** (Los Angeles, 1965), Newark, and **Detroit** (1967). Johnson's **Kerner Commission** (1968) concluded: "Our nation is moving toward **two societies, one black, one white—separate and unequal**," and blamed white racism.

**Malcolm X**, a minister of the **Nation of Islam**, rejected integration and nonviolence and stressed Black self-defense and pride. He later broke with the Nation and was assassinated in 1965. SNCC's **Stokely Carmichael** popularized **"Black Power"** (1966): racial pride, self-reliance, and Black control of Black communities. The **Black Panther Party** (Oakland, 1966; Huey Newton and Bobby Seale) organized armed patrols to watch police and ran free breakfast programs.

On **April 4, 1968**, **King was assassinated** in Memphis, and riots followed in over 100 cities. Days later Congress passed the **Civil Rights Act of 1968** (the **Fair Housing Act**). In 1971 the Court approved **busing** to desegregate schools (**Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg**).

## 4. Kennedy and Johnson
**John F. Kennedy** narrowly beat Richard Nixon in **1960**. Nixon was hurt in the first **televised debates**, and Kennedy became the first Catholic president. JFK's **"New Frontier"** produced the **Peace Corps** and a pledge to land on the **moon**; **Apollo 11** did it in 1969. Most of his domestic agenda stalled in Congress. He was **assassinated in Dallas** on **November 22, 1963**.

JFK's Cold War crises:
- The CIA-planned **Bay of Pigs** invasion of Cuba by exiles (April 1961) failed.
- The **Berlin Wall** went up (August 1961).
- In the **Cuban Missile Crisis** (October 1962), the U.S. discovered Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba. Kennedy imposed a naval **"quarantine"** (blockade). After 13 tense days, **Khrushchev removed the missiles** in exchange for a U.S. pledge not to invade Cuba and a secret agreement to remove U.S. missiles from Turkey. It was the **closest the world came to nuclear war**.
- Afterward came a **hotline** between Washington and Moscow and the **Limited Test Ban Treaty** (1963), which banned above-ground nuclear tests.
- Kennedy replaced massive retaliation with **"flexible response,"** which included more conventional and counterinsurgency forces such as the Green Berets.

**Lyndon B. Johnson** won a landslide over conservative Republican **Barry Goldwater** in **1964**. He then launched the **Great Society**, inspired partly by **Michael Harrington's** *The Other America* (1962), which exposed hidden poverty. Its main pieces:
- **War on Poverty** (Economic Opportunity Act, 1964): **Job Corps**, VISTA, community action programs, and **Head Start** for preschoolers.
- **Medicare** (health insurance for the **elderly**) and **Medicaid** (for the **poor**), 1965.
- The **Elementary and Secondary Education Act** (1965): the first major federal aid to public schools.
- The **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965** (Hart-Celler) **abolished the national-origins quotas** of 1924. Immigration from **Asia and Latin America** grew dramatically as a result.
- New departments (Housing and Urban Development, Transportation), the **National Endowments for the Arts and Humanities**, and public broadcasting.

Johnson also appointed **Thurgood Marshall**, the first Black Supreme Court justice (1967). Poverty fell sharply in the late 1960s, but the war in Vietnam drained money and attention from the Great Society.

## 5. The Warren Court (1953–1969)
Chief Justice **Earl Warren's** Court expanded rights dramatically:
- **Mapp v. Ohio** (1961): illegally obtained evidence is excluded from state trials.
- **Engel v. Vitale** (1962): official **school prayer** is unconstitutional.
- **Gideon v. Wainwright** (1963): states must provide **lawyers** to poor defendants.
- **Reynolds v. Sims** (1964), following Baker v. Carr: **"one person, one vote"** in legislative districts.
- **Griswold v. Connecticut** (1965): a right to **privacy** covering contraception.
- **Miranda v. Arizona** (1966): police must inform suspects of their **rights**.
- **Loving v. Virginia** (1967): struck down bans on **interracial marriage**.
- **Tinker v. Des Moines** (1969): students' antiwar armbands are protected speech.

Conservatives accused the Court of "judicial activism."

## 6. Other rights movements
- **Women:** **Betty Friedan's** *The Feminine Mystique* (1963) described the "problem that has no name," the discontent of educated suburban housewives. Friedan co-founded the **National Organization for Women (NOW)** in 1966.
  - The **Equal Pay Act** (1963) and **Title VII** opened legal doors.
  - Radical "women's liberation" groups raised consciousness, and **Gloria Steinem** founded *Ms.* magazine (1972).
  - Congress passed the **Equal Rights Amendment** in 1972. **Phyllis Schlafly** led opposition, and it fell short of ratification.
  - **Title IX** (1972) banned sex discrimination in federally funded education, transforming women's sports.
  - **Roe v. Wade** (1973) legalized abortion nationwide.
- **Latinos:** **César Chávez** and **Dolores Huerta's** **United Farm Workers** used strikes and a national **grape boycott** (1965–70) to win contracts for migrant farmworkers. The **Chicano movement** demanded political power and bilingual education.
- **Native Americans:** The **American Indian Movement** (AIM, 1968) and others **occupied Alcatraz** (1969–71) and **Wounded Knee** (1973). Policy shifted to tribal **self-determination** (1975 act).
- **Gay rights:** the **Stonewall riots** (New York, 1969), sparked by a police raid on a gay bar, launched the modern gay liberation movement.
- **Environment:** **Rachel Carson's** *Silent Spring* (1962) exposed the dangers of the pesticide **DDT** and launched modern environmentalism. The first **Earth Day** was held in 1970.
- **Consumers:** **Ralph Nader's** *Unsafe at Any Speed* (1965) attacked auto safety.

## 7. The Vietnam War
**Roots:** After the French defeat (1954), the U.S. backed **Ngo Dinh Diem's** South Vietnam against **Ho Chi Minh's** communist North and the southern guerrillas, the **Viet Cong**. Policy rested on containment and the **domino theory**. Kennedy raised the number of military "advisers" to about 16,000. Diem, unpopular for persecuting Buddhists, was overthrown and killed in a coup in November 1963.

**Escalation:** In August 1964, North Vietnamese boats reportedly attacked U.S. destroyers. The reports were murky, and the second attack probably never happened. Congress passed the **Gulf of Tonkin Resolution** almost unanimously. It gave Johnson a **"blank check"** to use force without a declaration of war. In 1965 he began sustained bombing (**Operation Rolling Thunder**) and sent ground combat troops. U.S. forces peaked at about **536,000** in 1968. General **William Westmoreland** pursued a **war of attrition**: "search and destroy" missions measured by **body counts**. Officials' optimistic claims created a **"credibility gap."**

**The turning point:** In the **Tet Offensive** (January 1968), communist forces attacked cities across South Vietnam, even the U.S. embassy grounds in Saigon. Militarily it was a **defeat for the communists**, but it was a **political and psychological victory**: it shattered claims that the war was nearly won. Respected TV anchor Walter Cronkite called the war a stalemate. In March, Johnson announced he would **not seek reelection**.

**1968, year of shocks:** King was assassinated in April. **Robert Kennedy** was assassinated in June while running for president. Police clashed with antiwar protesters at the **Democratic convention in Chicago**. **Richard Nixon** won by appealing to the **"silent majority"** and **"law and order."** Third-party candidate George Wallace carried five Deep South states.

**Nixon's war:**
- **Vietnamization**: gradually withdrawing U.S. troops while building up South Vietnamese forces.
- The **Nixon Doctrine**: allies must supply their own troops.
- He secretly bombed and then invaded **Cambodia** (1970), setting off campus protests. National Guardsmen killed four students at **Kent State** and police killed two at **Jackson State**.
- The **My Lai massacre** (1968, revealed in 1969), in which U.S. troops killed hundreds of unarmed villagers, deepened disillusionment.
- **Daniel Ellsberg** leaked the **Pentagon Papers** (1971), a secret history showing that officials had misled the public. The Supreme Court allowed the *New York Times* to publish them.
- The **Paris Peace Accords** (January 1973) withdrew U.S. forces. The **draft ended** in 1973.
- **Saigon fell** to North Vietnam in **April 1975**.

About **58,000 Americans** and millions of Vietnamese died. Congress reasserted itself with the **War Powers Resolution** (1973), which requires the president to notify Congress within 48 hours of sending troops and to withdraw them within 60 days without authorization. The **Twenty-sixth Amendment** (1971) lowered the **voting age to 18**: if you were old enough to be drafted, you were old enough to vote.

## 8. The New Left and the counterculture
- **Students for a Democratic Society** (SDS) issued the **Port Huron Statement** (1962), which called for **"participatory democracy."**
- The **Free Speech Movement** at **Berkeley** (1964) launched campus activism.
- The antiwar movement grew from **teach-ins** to mass marches and draft-card burnings.
- The **counterculture** of **"hippies"** rejected middle-class norms: long hair, rock music, drugs, communes, and the 1967 "Summer of Love" in San Francisco's Haight-Ashbury. It culminated at **Woodstock** (1969).
- Folk and rock musicians such as **Bob Dylan** ("The Times They Are a-Changin'") gave the era its soundtrack.
- Many Americans recoiled from the counterculture, which fed the conservative backlash that elected Nixon and later Reagan.
