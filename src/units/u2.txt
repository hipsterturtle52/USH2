## The big picture
Between the Civil War and 1900 the United States became the world's leading **industrial economy**. Railroads, steel, oil, and electricity built enormous fortunes. They also brought dangerous factories, crowded cities, and violent strikes. Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner called it **"The Gilded Age"** (1873): a thin layer of gold over serious problems. This unit has four pieces: **big business**, **labor**, **immigrants and cities**, and **the West**. A short section on **politics** follows.

## 1. Railroads and big business
The **transcontinental railroad** was finished at **Promontory Point, Utah, in 1869**. The Union Pacific was built largely by Irish workers and the Central Pacific by Chinese workers. Congress subsidized railroads with huge **land grants**. Railroads created a **national market** and drove demand for steel and coal. They even led to **standard time zones** (1883).

New business strategies created giant corporations:
- **Andrew Carnegie** (steel) used the **Bessemer process** and **vertical integration**: he owned every stage of production, from iron mines and ore ships to mills and railroads. In 1901 he sold to banker **J.P. Morgan**, who created **U.S. Steel**, the first **billion-dollar corporation**.
- **John D. Rockefeller** (**Standard Oil**) used **horizontal integration**. He bought out or crushed competing refiners until he controlled about 90% of U.S. refining. He organized the business as a **trust** (1882), in which stockholders of many companies handed control to one board of trustees. Later, **holding companies** did the same job.
- Critics called these men **"robber barons"**. Admirers called them **"captains of industry."**

New inventions remade daily life. **Alexander Graham Bell** invented the telephone (1876). **Thomas Edison** set up a research lab at Menlo Park and produced a practical **light bulb** (1879). **George Westinghouse** promoted alternating current.

!cec Railroads + new technology + cheap immigrant labor + government support | Rise of giant corporations and trusts | Huge wealth gap, monopoly power, and a backlash from farmers and workers

### Ideas that justified (and challenged) big business
**Social Darwinism** applied "survival of the fittest" to society. **Herbert Spencer** and Yale's **William Graham Sumner** argued that the rich had earned their place and government should not help the poor. This fit with **laissez-faire** economics. **Horatio Alger** novels sold the "rags to riches" dream.

Carnegie's **"Gospel of Wealth"** (1889) argued that the rich had a duty to give their fortunes away for the public good, through libraries, universities, and concert halls. "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced."

Critics pushed back:
- **Henry George**, *Progress and Poverty* (1879), proposed a **"single tax"** on land values.
- **Edward Bellamy**, *Looking Backward* (1888), imagined a cooperative socialist utopia in the year 2000.
- **Thorstein Veblen** coined **"conspicuous consumption"** for the wasteful display of the rich (*The Theory of the Leisure Class*, 1899).

### First steps at regulation
Farmers angry at railroad rates won state **"Granger laws."** In **Munn v. Illinois** (1877), the Supreme Court upheld state regulation of businesses "clothed with a public interest," in that case grain elevators. But **Wabash v. Illinois** (1886) ruled that states could not regulate **interstate** commerce. Congress responded with two laws:
- the **Interstate Commerce Act** (1887), which created the **Interstate Commerce Commission**, the first federal regulatory agency
- the **Sherman Antitrust Act** (1890), which outlawed combinations "in restraint of trade"

Both were weak at first. In **U.S. v. E.C. Knight** (1895), the Court ruled that a sugar trust controlling 98% of refining was *manufacturing*, not *commerce*, so the Sherman Act did not reach it. Ironically, the Sherman Act was used more against **labor unions** than against trusts in the 1890s.

## 2. Labor
Factory work meant **12-hour days**, six-day weeks, child labor, dangerous machines, and no compensation for injuries. Workers organized in two main ways:

- The **Knights of Labor** (led by **Terence Powderly**) welcomed nearly everyone: skilled and unskilled workers, women, and Black workers. It favored the **eight-hour day** and cooperatives. It collapsed after being blamed for the Haymarket bombing.
- The **American Federation of Labor (AFL, 1886)**, led by **Samuel Gompers**, was a federation of **skilled craft unions**. It practiced **"bread-and-butter" unionism**: higher wages, shorter hours, better conditions. It used strikes and collective bargaining and avoided radical politics. The AFL survived and grew.

The great strikes are tested often. Know who, what, and the result:
- **Great Railroad Strike (1877):** the first nationwide strike, set off by wage cuts. President Hayes sent **federal troops**.
- **Haymarket Square (Chicago, 1886):** a bomb exploded at a rally for the eight-hour day. Anarchists were blamed, and a wave of **anti-union, anti-immigrant** feeling followed.
- **Homestead Strike (1892):** at Carnegie's steel works, manager **Henry Clay Frick** hired **Pinkerton** guards. A gun battle followed, the state militia broke the strike, and the steelworkers' union was crushed.
- **Pullman Strike (1894):** **Eugene V. Debs's American Railway Union** boycotted Pullman cars and paralyzed the railroads. President **Cleveland** sent troops, using a federal court **injunction**, on the grounds that the strike interfered with the mail. Debs went to jail and later became a **Socialist**.

!cec Wage cuts and harsh conditions | Major strikes (1877, 1886, 1892, 1894) | Government sides with employers: troops, injunctions; public links unions to radicalism

## 3. Immigrants and cities
After about 1880, the **"New Immigrants"** came mostly from **southern and eastern Europe**: Italians, Poles, Russian Jews, Greeks, and Slavs. Most were Catholic, Orthodox, or Jewish, and many were poor. They entered through **Ellis Island** (opened 1892). Asian immigrants on the West Coast later passed through **Angel Island** (1910).

**Nativism** (hostility to immigrants) grew. The **Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)** was the **first federal law to bar immigrants by nationality**. The anti-Catholic **American Protective Association** (1887) organized against the newcomers.

Cities exploded. In 1870 about one American in four lived in an urban area. By 1920 it was more than half. Skyscrapers (architect **Louis Sullivan**: "form follows function"), electric streetcars, and the **Brooklyn Bridge** (1883) changed the landscape. The poor crowded into **tenements**. Photojournalist **Jacob Riis** exposed them in *How the Other Half Lives* (1890).

**Political machines** such as New York's **Tammany Hall**, run by **"Boss" William Tweed**, traded jobs, food, and favors for immigrant votes, and stole millions. Cartoonist **Thomas Nast** helped bring Tweed down.

Reformers responded:
- **Settlement houses**, above all **Jane Addams's Hull House** (Chicago, 1889), offered classes, child care, and English lessons, and pushed for reform laws.
- The **Social Gospel** movement (**Walter Rauschenbusch**, Washington Gladden) urged churches to fix social problems on earth.
- The **Woman's Christian Temperance Union** (**Frances Willard**) fought alcohol and became the largest women's organization of the era.

## 4. The West
The **Homestead Act** (1862) offered **160 acres** of public land to settlers who farmed it for five years. The **Morrill Land-Grant Act** (1862) funded state agricultural and technical colleges. Mining booms, **cattle drives** up trails such as the Chisholm Trail (many cowboys were Black or Mexican), and finally **barbed wire** (1874) closed the open range.

The cost fell on Plains Indians. Hunters slaughtered the **buffalo**, the base of Plains life, and the herds nearly vanished by the late 1880s. The wars ran from the **Sand Creek Massacre** (1864) through these events:
- **Little Bighorn (1876):** Lakota and Cheyenne under **Sitting Bull** and **Crazy Horse** wiped out **Custer's** force.
- **Chief Joseph** and the Nez Perce attempted a long flight toward Canada and surrendered in 1877: "I will fight no more forever."
- **Wounded Knee (1890):** the army massacred about 250 Lakota after alarm over the **Ghost Dance** religion. It marked the end of armed resistance.

Writer **Helen Hunt Jackson** condemned federal treatment of Native peoples in *A Century of Dishonor* (1881). But reformers' main answer was **assimilation**. The **Dawes Severalty Act (1887)** broke up reservations into **individual allotments** (160 acres per family head) and promised citizenship. "Surplus" land was sold to whites, and Native Americans lost about **two-thirds** of their land by 1934. Boarding schools such as **Carlisle** (founded by Richard Henry Pratt) forced Native children to give up their languages and cultures.

In 1893 historian **Frederick Jackson Turner** argued in his **"frontier thesis"** that the frontier had shaped American democracy and individualism. The 1890 census had just declared the frontier **closed**.

## 5. Gilded Age politics
Politics was a close, intense game. Elections were narrow, **voter turnout was very high** (often about 80%), and the two parties differed less on policy than on loyalty, region, religion, and **patronage**. Debate focused on **tariffs**, **currency**, and **civil service**.

In 1881 a disappointed office-seeker assassinated President **James Garfield**. This led to the **Pendleton Civil Service Act (1883)**, which filled some federal jobs by **merit exams** instead of the **spoils system**. In 1884, reform Republicans called **"Mugwumps"** bolted to Democrat **Grover Cleveland**, the only president to serve two **non-consecutive** terms (1885–89, 1893–97) until Donald Trump repeated the feat in 2025.

Diplomacy was quiet. The **Treaty of Washington** (1871) sent the **Alabama claims** against Britain (over damage by Confederate raiders built in British shipyards) to arbitration, which awarded the U.S. $15.5 million. Secretary of State **James G. Blaine** hosted the first **Pan-American Conference** (1889) to expand U.S. influence and trade in Latin America.

## Culture in brief
Writers turned to **realism**: **Mark Twain** (*Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*, 1884), William Dean Howells, and Henry James. Then came **naturalism**, which showed people at the mercy of their environment: **Stephen Crane** (*Maggie: A Girl of the Streets*), Jack London, and **Theodore Dreiser** (*Sister Carrie*). Painters **Winslow Homer** and **Thomas Eakins** worked in a realist style. **Public high schools**, land-grant colleges, research universities such as **Johns Hopkins** (1876), and women's colleges expanded quickly. The **Chautauqua** movement brought adult education to small towns. Mass leisure arrived: **baseball**, vaudeville, amusement parks, department stores, and mail-order catalogs from **Montgomery Ward** and **Sears**.
