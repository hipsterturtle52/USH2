## The big question
In 1865 the Union had won the war, and about four million enslaved people were free. Two questions followed. **On what terms would the eleven Confederate states rejoin the Union?** And **what would freedom actually mean for formerly enslaved people?** The struggle over these questions is called **Reconstruction** (1865–1877). The short version: freedpeople won major constitutional rights during Reconstruction. Then the North lost interest, white southern Democrats retook power, and most of those rights were stripped away by 1900.

## Presidential Reconstruction (1865–1866)
Lincoln had planned a lenient restoration. Under his **Ten Percent Plan** (1863), a state could form a new government once 10% of its 1860 voters swore loyalty to the Union. Congress pushed back with the harsher **Wade-Davis Bill**, which required 50% and an "ironclad oath," but Lincoln pocket-vetoed it. Lincoln was assassinated in April 1865, and **Andrew Johnson**, a pro-Union Democrat from Tennessee, became president.

Johnson pardoned thousands of ex-Confederates and let the old southern elite rebuild state governments. Those governments passed **Black Codes**. These laws limited freedpeople's right to own or rent land, required them to sign yearly labor contracts, and let authorities arrest unemployed Black men for "vagrancy" and hire them out. Johnson also returned land confiscated during the war to its prewar owners. That ended the hopes raised by General Sherman's Field Order No. 15 that freedpeople would get **"forty acres and a mule."**

!cec Johnson's lenient pardons; old elites back in power | Southern states pass Black Codes; anti-Black riots in Memphis and New Orleans (1866) | Northern voters elect a Republican supermajority in 1866; Congress takes over Reconstruction

The **Thirteenth Amendment** (ratified December 1865) abolished slavery. Congress also created the **Freedmen's Bureau** (1865). It provided food and medical care and supervised labor contracts. Its most lasting work was **education**: with northern missionary societies, it helped found schools and colleges such as **Howard** and **Fisk**.

## Congressional (Radical) Reconstruction (1867–1877)
The **Radical Republicans**, led by **Thaddeus Stevens** in the House and **Charles Sumner** in the Senate, wanted to remake southern society and protect Black rights. Congress passed the **Civil Rights Act of 1866** over Johnson's veto. Then, to put those rights beyond the reach of future majorities, it wrote the **Fourteenth Amendment** (ratified 1868). The amendment made everyone born in the U.S. a **citizen** and barred states from denying anyone **"due process"** or **"equal protection of the laws."** It later became the foundation of the twentieth-century civil rights revolution.

When southern states refused to ratify it, Congress passed the **Reconstruction Acts of 1867**. These acts divided the former Confederacy (except Tennessee, already readmitted) into **five military districts**. To be readmitted, a state had to write a new constitution guaranteeing **Black male suffrage** and ratify the Fourteenth Amendment.

Congress also passed the **Tenure of Office Act**, which barred the president from removing certain officials without Senate approval. Johnson fired Secretary of War Edwin Stanton anyway, and in 1868 the House **impeached** him. The Senate fell **one vote** short of removing him. Johnson finished his term with little power left.

**Ulysses S. Grant** won the presidency in 1868. The **Fifteenth Amendment** (1870) barred denying the vote on account of **"race, color, or previous condition of servitude."** It did not mention sex, which angered woman suffrage leaders and split their movement.

### Reconstruction governments in the South
Southern Republican governments drew support from three groups:
- freedmen, who voted in large numbers
- **"carpetbaggers"**, northerners who moved South
- **"scalawags"**, white southerners who joined the Republicans

About 2,000 Black men held office. **Hiram Revels** of Mississippi became the **first African American U.S. senator** (1870). These governments created the South's first **public school systems** and rebuilt railroads, but they also ran up debt and suffered some corruption, which opponents exaggerated.

Freedpeople built independent lives. They **reunited families**, legalized marriages, and founded **Black churches**, which became the center of community and political life. They also flocked to schools.

### Violence and retreat
The **Ku Klux Klan** (founded 1866) and similar groups used terror to keep Black men from voting. Congress answered with the **Enforcement Acts** and the **Ku Klux Klan Act** (1870–71), which let federal troops and courts crush the first Klan. But northern commitment faded for several reasons:
- the **Panic of 1873** turned voters' attention to the economy
- scandals in Grant's administration, including the **Crédit Mobilier** railroad bribery scheme and the **Whiskey Ring** tax fraud, discredited Republican rule
- northern racism made voters weary of the "southern question"

The Supreme Court also narrowed the new amendments. In the **Slaughterhouse Cases** (1873), the Court read the Fourteenth Amendment's protections narrowly. **U.S. v. Cruikshank** (1876) overturned convictions from the **Colfax Massacre** and left protection of Black citizens mostly to the states.

## The end of Reconstruction: the Compromise of 1877
The **election of 1876** between **Rutherford B. Hayes** (Republican) and **Samuel Tilden** (Democrat) hinged on disputed electoral votes from three southern states. In the informal **Compromise of 1877**, Democrats accepted Hayes as president, and Hayes **withdrew the last federal troops** from southern statehouses. Southern Democrats who called themselves **"Redeemers"** took over every southern state.

## The "New South" and Jim Crow (1877–1900)
Atlanta editor **Henry Grady** promoted a **"New South"** of factories, railroads, and diversified farming. Some industry did grow: **textile mills**, **Birmingham steel**, and **tobacco** (James B. Duke's American Tobacco Company). But the South stayed poor and mostly agricultural, and northern capital often owned its industry.

### The farm trap
Without land, most freedpeople, and many poor whites, became **sharecroppers**. They farmed a landowner's land in exchange for a share of the crop, often half. Others were **tenant farmers** who rented land. Under the **crop-lien system**, local merchants supplied seed and food on credit, secured by a lien on the coming crop. High interest and low cotton prices kept families in **permanent debt**.

### Jim Crow and disfranchisement
By the 1890s southern states built a system of legal segregation known as **Jim Crow**. To get around the Fifteenth Amendment, they used:
- **poll taxes**
- **literacy tests**, applied unfairly by white registrars
- **white primaries**
- **grandfather clauses**, which excused a man from these tests if his grandfather could vote before 1867, so poor whites kept the vote

The Supreme Court went along:
- **Civil Rights Cases (1883):** struck down the Civil Rights Act of 1875, holding that the Fourteenth Amendment limits *state* action, not private businesses.
- **Plessy v. Ferguson (1896):** upheld segregated railroad cars under the doctrine of **"separate but equal."** Justice **John Marshall Harlan** dissented alone: "Our Constitution is color-blind."

**Lynching** peaked in the 1890s. Journalist **Ida B. Wells** exposed it in pamphlets such as *Southern Horrors* (1892). Some Black southerners left. The **Exodusters** moved to Kansas in 1879.

### Two Black strategies
**Booker T. Washington** founded the **Tuskegee Institute** (1881). In his **Atlanta Compromise** speech (1895), he urged Black southerners to accept segregation for now and focus on **vocational education** and economic self-help. **W.E.B. Du Bois** answered in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903). He demanded **full civil and political rights now** and higher education for a **"Talented Tenth"** of leaders. (Du Bois's later work is in Unit 3.)

Meanwhile, white southerners built the **"Lost Cause"** memory of the war. It cast the Confederacy as a noble fight for states' rights rather than slavery. Monuments and textbooks spread this view for generations.

### One diplomatic footnote
In 1867 Secretary of State **William Seward** bought **Alaska** from Russia for $7.2 million. Critics mocked it as "Seward's Folly."

+ the Fourteenth Amendment barred discrimination by states but not by private individuals and businesses || This "state action" reasoning struck down the Civil Rights Act of 1875's ban on discrimination in inns, theaters, and railroads.
- segregated public schools were unconstitutional || That was Brown v. Board of Education (1954).
