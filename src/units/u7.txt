## The big picture
World War II left two superpowers with opposite systems. The **United States** stood for capitalism and liberal democracy. The **Soviet Union** stood for communism and one-party rule. Their rivalry, the **Cold War**, shaped American foreign policy for 45 years. It also shaped life at home: a **second Red Scare**, a permanent military buildup, and federal money for highways, science, and schools. At the same time, the 1950s brought **unprecedented prosperity**: the **baby boom**, **suburbs**, **television**, and a consumer culture that critics attacked as conformist.

## 1. How the Cold War began (1945–1949)
The Soviets had lost over 20 million people and wanted a **buffer zone** of friendly states. At Yalta, Stalin had promised free elections in Eastern Europe, but he installed **communist governments** in Poland, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, and (in 1948) Czechoslovakia. In 1946 **Winston Churchill** warned that an **"iron curtain"** had descended across Europe.

American diplomat **George Kennan** argued in his **"Long Telegram"** (1946) and his **"X" article** (1947) that Soviet expansion must be met with patient, firm **containment**. Containment became the core of U.S. policy:
- **Truman Doctrine** (March 1947): Truman asked Congress for **$400 million** for **Greece and Turkey**, declaring that the U.S. must "support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation."
- **Marshall Plan** (1948): about **$13 billion** in economic aid to rebuild Western Europe, so that poverty would not breed communism. The Soviets refused it for their bloc.
- **Berlin Blockade and Airlift** (1948–49): Stalin cut off land access to West Berlin, and the U.S. and Britain **flew in supplies for nearly a year** until he backed down.
- **NATO** (1949): the first U.S. **peacetime military alliance**, built on the idea that an attack on one is an attack on all. The Soviet bloc answered with the **Warsaw Pact** (1955).
- The **National Security Act** (1947) created the **Department of Defense**, the **CIA**, and the **National Security Council**.

1949 was a shock year. The Soviets tested an **atomic bomb**, and **Mao Zedong's** communists won China's civil war. Republicans charged that Truman had "lost China." A secret report, **NSC-68** (1950), called for a massive military buildup to fight communism everywhere.

!cec Soviet control of Eastern Europe; fear of communist expansion | Containment: Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, NATO | A permanent U.S. global military presence and an arms race

## 2. The Korean War (1950–1953)
In **June 1950**, communist **North Korea** invaded **South Korea**. The **UN Security Council** authorized a defense; the Soviets were boycotting it and could not veto. The war unfolded in stages:
- **General Douglas MacArthur's** landing at **Inchon** turned the tide, and UN forces drove north toward China's border at the Yalu River.
- **China** entered with hundreds of thousands of troops and pushed them back.
- MacArthur publicly demanded a wider war, including bombing China. Truman **fired him** in April 1951 for insubordination, upholding **civilian control of the military**.
- After two years of stalemate, an **armistice (1953)** left Korea divided near the **38th parallel**, where it had started.

Korea was the first "**limited war**" of the Cold War, fought for containment rather than total victory. About 36,000 Americans died.

## 3. The Second Red Scare
Fear of communist subversion swept the country:
- Truman's **loyalty program** (1947) screened federal employees.
- The **House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC)** investigated Hollywood. The **"Hollywood Ten"** were jailed for refusing to testify, and a **blacklist** cost hundreds of people their jobs.
- **Alger Hiss**, a former State Department official accused of spying, was convicted of **perjury** (1950). The case made Congressman **Richard Nixon** famous.
- **Julius and Ethel Rosenberg** were executed (1953) for passing atomic secrets to the Soviets.
- The **McCarran Internal Security Act** (1950) required communist organizations to register.

In February 1950, Senator **Joseph McCarthy** of Wisconsin claimed to have a list of communists in the State Department. He never proved a single case, but **"McCarthyism"**, meaning reckless accusations of disloyalty, ruined many careers. His downfall came in the televised **Army-McCarthy hearings** (1954). Army lawyer **Joseph Welch** asked, "Have you no sense of decency, sir?" The **Senate censured** McCarthy in December 1954. Playwright **Arthur Miller's** *The Crucible* (1953) used the Salem witch trials as an allegory for the hunt.

## 4. Truman at home
- **Reconversion:** a massive **strike wave** (1946) and inflation followed the end of price controls.
- The **Employment Act** (1946) made maximum employment a federal goal and created the **Council of Economic Advisers**.
- The Republican Congress passed the **Taft-Hartley Act** (1947) over Truman's veto. It **banned the closed shop**, allowed states to pass **"right-to-work" laws**, permitted 80-day "cooling-off" injunctions in national-emergency strikes, and required union leaders to swear they were not communists.
- **Civil rights:** Truman's committee report *To Secure These Rights* (1947) called for federal action. In 1948 Truman issued **Executive Order 9981**, **desegregating the armed forces**. That same year, **Jackie Robinson** had broken baseball's color line with the Brooklyn Dodgers (1947).
- **Election of 1948:** Truman faced a three-way split. Southern **Dixiecrats** (**Strom Thurmond**) walked out over civil rights, and **Henry Wallace's** Progressives attacked Truman from the left. Truman won a famous **upset** over Thomas Dewey anyway.
- **Fair Deal:** Truman won a higher minimum wage, expanded Social Security, and public housing, but Congress rejected **national health insurance**.
- The **Twenty-second Amendment** (1951) limited presidents to **two terms**.

## 5. Eisenhower: the middle way
**Dwight Eisenhower** (1953–61) practiced **"Modern Republicanism."** He accepted and even expanded the New Deal: Social Security grew, and he created the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. But he favored balanced budgets. The **Interstate Highway Act** (1956), justified partly for defense and evacuation, built 41,000 miles of highway, the largest public works program in history.

**Foreign policy:** Secretary of State **John Foster Dulles** practiced **brinkmanship**.
- The **"New Look"** relied on nuclear weapons and the threat of **"massive retaliation"** rather than costly ground forces: "more bang for the buck."
- The **CIA** ran **covert operations**, overthrowing governments in **Iran** (1953) and **Guatemala** (1954).
- Eisenhower's **domino theory** (1954) held that if Vietnam fell to communism, its neighbors would follow. After the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu, the **Geneva Accords** (1954) temporarily divided Vietnam at the **17th parallel**. The U.S. backed **Ngo Dinh Diem's** South Vietnam and formed **SEATO**.
- In the **Suez Crisis** (1956), Eisenhower forced Britain, France, and Israel to withdraw from Egypt.
- He did **not** intervene when the Soviets crushed the **Hungarian Revolution** (1956).
- The **Eisenhower Doctrine** (1957) offered aid against communism in the **Middle East**.

**Sputnik:** In October 1957 the Soviets launched **Sputnik**, the first artificial satellite. Americans feared a "missile gap" and a science gap. Congress created **NASA** (1958) and passed the **National Defense Education Act** (1958), which funded science, math, and foreign-language education.

At the end of his term, the **U-2 incident** (1960), a U.S. spy plane shot down over the USSR, wrecked a summit. In 1959 **Fidel Castro** took power in Cuba. In his **Farewell Address** (1961), Eisenhower warned against the **"military-industrial complex."**

!cec Sputnik (1957) | NASA, National Defense Education Act, space race | Federal investment in science and education; Cold War competition reaches schools

## 6. The affluent society
The postwar economy boomed. Pent-up savings, government spending (defense, highways, the **GI Bill**), and cheap energy fueled it. Births soared in the **baby boom** (1946–1964).

**Suburbs:** Builder **William Levitt** mass-produced affordable homes in **Levittown**, New York. **FHA and VA mortgages** made them easy to buy. But **restrictive covenants and "redlining"** excluded Black families, which cemented residential segregation. Millions moved to suburbs and to the **Sun Belt**, while inner cities lost people and tax revenue.

**Consumer culture:** Families bought **cars**, appliances, and **television**. By 1960 almost 90% of households had a TV, which spread shared programs and advertising. Credit cards appeared. A **religious revival** (evangelist **Billy Graham**) went with Cold War anti-communism: "under God" was added to the Pledge of Allegiance (1954). **Jonas Salk's polio vaccine** (1955) was a triumph of science.

**The ideal of domesticity:** Popular culture celebrated the suburban homemaker, and **Dr. Benjamin Spock's** *Baby and Child Care* (1946) became a bestseller. In reality, more married women were taking paid jobs every year.

**Critics of conformity:**
- **David Riesman**, *The Lonely Crowd* (1950): "other-directed" people seek approval from peers.
- **William Whyte**, *The Organization Man* (1956): corporate conformity.
- **John Kenneth Galbraith**, *The Affluent Society* (1958): private wealth amid public neglect.
- **C. Wright Mills**, *The Power Elite* (1956).
- Novels like *The Man in the Gray Flannel Suit* (Sloan Wilson).

**Rebels:** The **Beat Generation** rejected materialism and conformity: **Jack Kerouac's** *On the Road* (1957) and **Allen Ginsberg's** *Howl* (1956). **J.D. Salinger's** *The Catcher in the Rye* (1951) spoke to alienated teens. **Rock and roll** (Elvis Presley, Chuck Berry, Little Richard) blended Black rhythm and blues with country and alarmed parents. **Abstract Expressionist** painters such as **Jackson Pollock** made New York the center of the art world.

**Left behind:** Prosperity did not reach everyone. Federal Indian policy shifted to **"termination"** (ending tribal recognition) and **relocation** to cities. **"Operation Wetback"** (1954) deported large numbers of Mexican migrants. Rural and urban poverty persisted (see Unit 8).
