## The big picture
After 1968 the liberal consensus of the New Deal and Great Society came apart. Several blows hit at once:
- **Watergate** destroyed trust in the presidency.
- **Stagflation**, high inflation plus high unemployment, ended the long postwar boom.
- **Oil shocks** revealed U.S. dependence on foreign energy.
- **Defeat in Vietnam** and the **Iranian hostage crisis** dented confidence abroad.

Out of that frustration rose a **new conservative coalition** that elected **Ronald Reagan** in 1980. In foreign policy the period moved from **détente** (relaxing tensions with the USSR and China) to a renewed Cold War under Reagan, and then to the dramatic thaw with **Gorbachev** that set up the Cold War's end.

## 1. Nixon at home: more liberal than you'd think
Richard Nixon (1969–74) courted conservatives with a **"Southern strategy"** aimed at white southern voters, talk of the **"silent majority,"** and **"New Federalism,"** which returned money to the states through **revenue sharing**. But he also signed major regulatory laws:
- the **Environmental Protection Agency** (EPA, 1970)
- the **Clean Air Act** (1970)
- the **Occupational Safety and Health Administration** (OSHA)
- the **Endangered Species Act** (1973)

He proposed a guaranteed income, the Family Assistance Plan, which did not pass. He also began **affirmative action** requirements for federal contractors (the Philadelphia Plan).

**Economics:** Inflation from Vietnam spending plus slowing growth produced **stagflation**. In 1971 Nixon imposed temporary **wage and price controls** and **ended the dollar's convertibility to gold**. That effectively ended the **Bretton Woods** system of fixed exchange rates.

## 2. Nixon abroad: détente
With national security adviser (later Secretary of State) **Henry Kissinger**, Nixon practiced **realpolitik**: national interest over ideology. He exploited the **Sino-Soviet split**:
- **Nixon's visit to China** (February 1972) opened relations with the communist People's Republic, a stunning reversal by a lifelong anti-communist.
- In Moscow (May 1972) he signed **SALT I**, the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks agreement, which froze the number of offensive missiles, and the **Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty**. This relaxation of tensions was called **détente**.

When Egypt and Syria attacked Israel in the **Yom Kippur War** (1973), the U.S. airlifted aid to Israel. Arab members of **OPEC** responded with an **oil embargo** (1973–74). Oil prices **quadrupled**, gas lines formed, and the era of cheap energy ended.

!cec Vietnam spending + rising global competition + OPEC oil shock (1973) | Stagflation: inflation and unemployment rise together | Keynesian tools look broken; voters open to deregulation, tax cuts, and conservative economics

## 3. Watergate (1972–1974)
In June **1972**, burglars connected to Nixon's reelection committee were caught breaking into **Democratic National Committee headquarters** at the **Watergate** complex. Nixon won a **landslide** over George McGovern that November. But *Washington Post* reporters **Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein**, tipped by a secret source ("Deep Throat"), traced the burglary toward the White House. The crime was the **cover-up**: payments of hush money and obstruction of the FBI. The investigation unfolded in steps:
- **1973:** A **Senate committee** (Sam Ervin) held televised hearings. Former counsel **John Dean** testified against Nixon. An aide revealed that Nixon had **secretly taped** Oval Office conversations.
- **"Saturday Night Massacre"** (October 1973): Nixon ordered special prosecutor Archibald Cox fired. The attorney general and his deputy resigned rather than do it.
- **Separately**, Vice President **Spiro Agnew** resigned over bribery and tax evasion. Under the **Twenty-fifth Amendment**, Nixon appointed **Gerald Ford** vice president.
- **United States v. Nixon** (July 1974): the Supreme Court ruled unanimously that **executive privilege** did not shield the tapes. The "smoking gun" tape showed Nixon ordering the cover-up.
- The House Judiciary Committee approved **articles of impeachment**.
- On **August 9, 1974**, Nixon became the **only president to resign**.

**Ford** became president without being elected to either office. A month later he **pardoned Nixon**, which probably cost him the 1976 election.

**Aftermath:** Congress passed reforms:
- **campaign finance** limits (1974)
- a stronger **Freedom of Information Act**
- the **Ethics in Government Act** (1978), which created independent counsels
- the **Church Committee** (1975) exposed CIA and FBI abuses

Public **trust in government fell** and never fully recovered.

## 4. Ford and Carter: a time of limits
**Gerald Ford** (1974–77) faced inflation (his "Whip Inflation Now" buttons were mocked) and the **fall of Saigon** (1975). He signed the **Helsinki Accords** (1975), which accepted Europe's postwar borders in exchange for Soviet human rights pledges.

**Jimmy Carter** (1977–81), a born-again Baptist and Washington outsider from Georgia, made **human rights** the centerpiece of his foreign policy:
- The **Panama Canal Treaties** (1977) agreed to return the canal to Panama by 1999.
- The **Camp David Accords** (1978): Carter brokered peace between Egypt's **Anwar Sadat** and Israel's **Menachem Begin**, the first Arab–Israeli peace treaty (1979). It is his greatest achievement.
- He established full **diplomatic relations with China** (1979).
- After the **Soviet invasion of Afghanistan** (December 1979), he withdrew the SALT II treaty from the Senate, imposed a **grain embargo**, and **boycotted the 1980 Moscow Olympics**. Détente was dead.
- **The Iranian hostage crisis:** the 1979 **Iranian Revolution** toppled the U.S.-backed Shah and set off a second **oil shock**. In November 1979 militants seized the **U.S. embassy in Tehran** and held **52 Americans hostage for 444 days**. A rescue mission failed in April 1980. The hostages were released minutes after Reagan's inauguration in January 1981.

At home, inflation hit double digits. Carter's **"malaise" speech** (1979) blamed a national "crisis of confidence" and backfired. He created the Departments of **Energy** and **Education** and began **deregulation** (airlines, 1978). The **Three Mile Island** nuclear accident (1979) set back nuclear power. Fed chairman **Paul Volcker** (appointed 1979) raised **interest rates** sharply to crush inflation. That worked, at the cost of a severe recession in 1981–82.

## 5. Society in the 1970s
- **The "Me Decade"** (Tom Wolfe's phrase) and Christopher Lasch's *The Culture of Narcissism* (1979) described a turn inward toward self-fulfillment.
- **Rust Belt and Sun Belt:** **Deindustrialization** hit the Northeast and Midwest as factories closed, while population and political power shifted to the **Sun Belt**.
- **Tax revolt:** California's **Proposition 13** (1978) capped property taxes and inspired anti-tax movements nationwide.
- **Affirmative action:** In **Regents of the University of California v. Bakke** (1978), the Court ruled that **racial quotas** in admissions were unconstitutional but that **race could be one factor**.
- **Busing:** court-ordered busing provoked violent resistance in places like **Boston** (1974).
- **Roe v. Wade** (1973) energized a **pro-life movement**. **Phyllis Schlafly's** campaign helped defeat the **ERA** (it died in 1982).
- **Culture:** Alex Haley's *Roots* (TV miniseries, 1977) drew record audiences and sparked interest in family and ethnic heritage.

### The New Right
Several groups converged:
- **Evangelical Christians**, organized in **Jerry Falwell's Moral Majority** (1979), opposed abortion, the ERA, gay rights, and the ban on school prayer.
- **Neoconservatives**, former liberals who became hawkish on the Soviet Union and skeptical of the Great Society.
- **Free-market economists** such as Milton Friedman.
- Former Democrats, especially white southerners and blue-collar northerners known as **"Reagan Democrats"**, angry about taxes, crime, and cultural change.

## 6. The Reagan Revolution (1981–1989)
In 1980 **Ronald Reagan** asked voters, "Are you better off than you were four years ago?" and beat Carter decisively. "Government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem," he declared.

**Reaganomics** (**supply-side economics**) held that cutting taxes would spur investment and growth:
- The **Economic Recovery Tax Act** (1981) cut income tax rates about 25% over three years.
- He cut domestic spending and promoted **deregulation**.
- When **air traffic controllers** (PATCO) struck illegally in 1981, Reagan **fired them**, a blow to organized labor.
- After the 1981–82 recession, the economy boomed and inflation fell.
- But big tax cuts plus a huge **military buildup** produced record **budget deficits**, and the **national debt roughly tripled**.
- Income inequality widened. The 1987 stock market crash and the **savings and loan crisis** showed the risks of deregulation.

Reagan appointed **Sandra Day O'Connor**, the **first woman on the Supreme Court** (1981). He survived an assassination attempt (1981) and won a 49-state landslide in **1984** over Walter Mondale, whose running mate **Geraldine Ferraro** was the first woman on a major-party ticket. The **Immigration Reform and Control Act** (1986) gave **amnesty** to about 3 million undocumented immigrants while penalizing employers who hired undocumented workers. The **AIDS** epidemic, first identified in 1981, devastated gay communities, and critics faulted the administration's slow response.

### Reagan and the Cold War
Reagan called the USSR an **"evil empire"** (1983). He began the largest peacetime **military buildup** and proposed the **Strategic Defense Initiative** (SDI, "Star Wars"), a space-based missile shield. The **Reagan Doctrine** aided **anti-communist insurgents**: the mujahideen in **Afghanistan** and the **Contras** in **Nicaragua**. U.S. forces invaded **Grenada** (1983). A suicide bombing killed **241 U.S. servicemen** in **Beirut** (1983).

The **Iran-Contra affair** (revealed 1986) was the administration's major scandal. Officials secretly **sold arms to Iran**, hoping to free hostages in Lebanon, and **diverted the profits to the Contras**, defying a congressional ban (the **Boland Amendment**). National Security Council aide **Oliver North** and others were prosecuted. Reagan said he did not know of the diversion.

Then came the thaw. **Mikhail Gorbachev** (Soviet leader from 1985) pursued reforms: **glasnost** (openness) and **perestroika** (restructuring). After summits in Geneva and Reykjavik, the two leaders signed the **INF Treaty** (1987), the first treaty to **eliminate an entire class of nuclear weapons** (intermediate-range missiles). In Berlin, Reagan challenged: **"Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!"** (1987).
