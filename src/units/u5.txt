## The big picture
The Great Depression was the worst economic collapse in American history. By 1933 about **one worker in four was unemployed**, thousands of banks had failed, and national output had fallen by roughly a third. **Herbert Hoover** responded with limited, mostly voluntary measures. **Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal** created a much larger federal government that provided **relief, recovery, and reform** (the "**three R's**"). The New Deal did not end the Depression; World War II did. But it permanently changed what Americans expect from Washington: Social Security, bank deposit insurance, union rights, and federal economic management.

## 1. Causes of the Depression
The **stock market crash** of October 1929 (**"Black Tuesday,"** October 29) was the trigger, not the only cause. The underlying weaknesses were:
- **Unequal distribution of income.** Wages lagged behind productivity, so workers could not buy all the goods factories produced (**overproduction / underconsumption**).
- **Speculation and buying stocks "on margin"**, with borrowed money. When prices fell, margin calls forced more selling.
- **A weak banking system.** Thousands of small, unregulated banks had **no deposit insurance**, so **bank runs** spread panic. About 9,000 banks failed between 1930 and 1933.
- **A long farm depression** and heavy consumer debt from installment buying.
- **A fragile international economy.** War debts and reparations were recycled through American loans. When U.S. lending stopped, Europe collapsed too.
- **Policy mistakes.** The **Hawley-Smoot Tariff** (1930) raised rates to record highs, other nations retaliated, and world trade shrank. The Federal Reserve let the money supply contract.

!cec Unequal income, margin buying, weak banks, high tariffs | Crash of 1929 and banking panics (1930–33) | Mass unemployment (~25% in 1933), shrinking output, and a demand for federal action

## 2. Hoover's response
Hoover was not idle, but he believed in **"rugged individualism"** and **voluntarism**. He asked business leaders to keep wages up and urged private charity and local governments to handle relief. He opposed direct federal payments to individuals. His main tools:
- The **Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC, 1932)** lent money to **banks, railroads, and large businesses**, on the theory that aid would "trickle down."
- A one-year **moratorium** on war debt and reparations payments (1931).

Shantytowns of the homeless became known as **"Hoovervilles."** In 1932 the **Bonus Army**, about 15,000 to 20,000 World War I veterans demanding early payment of a promised bonus, camped in Washington. Hoover had the army, under **Douglas MacArthur**, drive them out with tanks and tear gas. Hoover's reputation collapsed, and **FDR** won the 1932 election in a landslide.

## 3. The First New Deal (1933–1934)
"The only thing we have to fear is fear itself," Roosevelt said in his inaugural address. Advised by his **"Brain Trust"** of professors, he moved fast during the **Hundred Days**. He explained his programs directly to the public in radio **"fireside chats."**

**Banking and finance (reform):**
- A national **bank holiday** and the **Emergency Banking Act** reopened sound banks under federal supervision.
- The **Glass-Steagall Act** (1933) separated commercial and investment banking and created the **FDIC**, which **insures bank deposits**. Bank runs essentially ended.
- The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC, 1934)** regulated the stock market.
- The U.S. went **off the gold standard**.

**Relief:**
- The **Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC)** put young men to work planting forests and building parks.
- The **Federal Emergency Relief Administration** (Harry Hopkins) gave grants to the states.
- The **Home Owners' Loan Corporation** refinanced mortgages.

**Recovery:**
- The **Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA)** **paid farmers to reduce production** to raise crop prices. Critics were horrified that crops and livestock were destroyed while people went hungry, and payments helped landowners more than tenants and sharecroppers.
- The **National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA)** created the **NRA**, which let industries write "codes of fair competition" setting prices, wages, and hours. Its **Section 7(a)** guaranteed workers the right to organize. NIRA also created the **Public Works Administration (PWA)**.
- The **Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA)** built dams to provide flood control and **cheap public electricity** to one of the poorest regions. It was the boldest experiment in government-owned regional planning.

Also in 1933, the **Twenty-first Amendment** repealed Prohibition.

## 4. Critics left and right
The **American Liberty League** (1934), backed by wealthy business leaders, called the New Deal socialism. Critics on the other side said it did too little:
- Louisiana senator **Huey Long's "Share Our Wealth"** plan proposed confiscating great fortunes to guarantee every family a home and an income. Long was assassinated in 1935.
- **Father Charles Coughlin**, a "radio priest," attacked bankers and drifted into antisemitism.
- **Dr. Francis Townsend** proposed $200 monthly pensions for the elderly, pressure that helped produce Social Security.

The **Supreme Court** struck down key First New Deal laws:
- **Schechter Poultry v. U.S.** (1935, the "sick chicken" case) killed the NIRA.
- **U.S. v. Butler** (1936) struck down the AAA's processing tax.

## 5. The Second New Deal (1935–1938)
Pushed by these critics and by the Court, Roosevelt moved toward **reform and security**:
- **Works Progress Administration (WPA)** (Hopkins): employed millions building roads, schools, and airports. Its **Federal Writers', Art, Theatre, and Music Projects** employed artists and writers.
- **Social Security Act (1935)**: **old-age pensions** funded by payroll taxes, **unemployment insurance**, and aid to dependent children and the disabled. At first it **excluded farm and domestic workers**, which left out many African Americans and women.
- **Wagner Act (National Labor Relations Act, 1935)**: guaranteed workers' right to **organize and bargain collectively** and created the **National Labor Relations Board (NLRB)**. It is labor's most important New Deal victory.
- **Rural Electrification Administration**: brought power lines to farms.
- **Revenue Act of 1935**: the "wealth tax" raised top income tax rates.
- **Fair Labor Standards Act (1938)**: the first federal **minimum wage**, a standard workweek (phased down to 40 hours) with overtime pay, and a ban on most **child labor**.

### Labor's surge
The **Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO)**, led by **John L. Lewis**, organized whole **industries**, skilled and unskilled together, in autos, steel, and rubber. This put it at odds with the craft-based AFL. The **sit-down strike** at General Motors in **Flint, Michigan** (1936–37) won recognition of the United Auto Workers. Union membership roughly tripled during the decade.

### Politics of the New Deal
Roosevelt won a landslide in 1936 with the **New Deal coalition**: urban workers, union members, immigrants and their children, Catholics and Jews, **African Americans** (who shifted from the party of Lincoln), white southerners, and intellectuals. This coalition kept Democrats dominant for decades.

Then Roosevelt overreached. His **"court-packing" plan** (1937) asked to add up to six new justices. Even allies called it an attack on judicial independence, and Congress rejected it. But the Court began upholding New Deal laws, starting with **West Coast Hotel v. Parrish** (1937), the "switch in time that saved nine."

Roosevelt cut spending in 1937, and the economy fell back into the **"Roosevelt recession"** (1937–38). This seemed to support British economist **John Maynard Keynes**, who argued that government **deficit spending** should replace missing private demand during downturns. By 1938 a conservative coalition of Republicans and southern Democrats had stalled new reforms. **Massive WWII spending** finally ended the Depression.

!cec Depression, plus pressure from the left and Court defeats | Second New Deal: WPA, Social Security, Wagner Act | Lasting welfare state, union power, the New Deal coalition, and a much larger federal role

## 6. Society in the Depression
- **The Dust Bowl.** Drought plus years of over-plowing on the southern Great Plains produced massive dust storms. Hundreds of thousands of **"Okies"** went west to California, where they found low-paid migrant work. **John Steinbeck's** *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939) told their story. **Dorothea Lange's** photograph "Migrant Mother," taken for the Farm Security Administration, became the Depression's iconic image.
- **African Americans** were often "last hired, first fired," and New Deal programs were sometimes administered in discriminatory ways, especially in the South. Still, FDR's informal **"Black Cabinet"** (including **Mary McLeod Bethune**) and **Eleanor Roosevelt's** advocacy brought new attention. In 1939 Eleanor Roosevelt helped arrange **Marian Anderson's** concert at the **Lincoln Memorial** after the DAR barred her from Constitution Hall. The **Scottsboro Boys** case (1931), nine Black youths falsely accused of rape in Alabama, exposed southern injustice.
- **Mexican Americans**: during the **repatriation** campaigns, hundreds of thousands of Mexicans and Mexican Americans, many of them U.S. citizens, were pressured or forced to leave for Mexico.
- **Native Americans**: the **Indian Reorganization Act (1934)**, pushed by **John Collier**, ended allotment and encouraged **tribal self-government**, reversing the Dawes Act.
- **Women**: **Frances Perkins** became the **first woman cabinet member** (Secretary of Labor). Many married women faced pressure to leave jobs to men.

## 7. Culture and diplomacy
Americans sought **escape** at the movies: *Gone with the Wind* and *The Wizard of Oz* (both 1939), and Disney's *Snow White*. They also listened to **radio** (Orson Welles's *War of the Worlds* broadcast of 1938 caused a stir), **swing** music, and comic books (Superman, 1938).

Other artists documented hard times:
- The **social realism** of Steinbeck and FSA photographers
- James Agee and Walker Evans's *Let Us Now Praise Famous Men*
- Richard Wright's *Native Son* (1940)
- **Regionalist** painting such as **Grant Wood's** *American Gothic*

Abroad, FDR announced the **Good Neighbor Policy** toward Latin America: no more armed intervention. The U.S. gave up its Platt Amendment rights in Cuba (1934). The U.S. also **recognized the Soviet Union** (1933).
