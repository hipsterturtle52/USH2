## The big picture
In the 1930s Americans were determined to stay out of another European war, and Congress passed strict **Neutrality Acts**. As Germany, Italy, and Japan conquered their neighbors, **Franklin Roosevelt** moved the country step by step from neutrality to **"arsenal of democracy"** to open war after **Pearl Harbor** (December 7, 1941). The war ended the Depression, transformed the home front, opened jobs to women and minorities, and saw the **internment of Japanese Americans**. It left the U.S. a **superpower** holding the **atomic bomb**.

## 1. Isolationism in the 1930s
Disillusionment with World War I ran deep. The **Nye Committee** (1934–36) suggested that bankers and arms makers, the **"merchants of death,"** had dragged the U.S. into that war for profit. Congress responded with the **Neutrality Acts** (1935–37):
- **no arms sales or loans** to nations at war
- no travel by Americans on belligerent ships
- after 1937, **"cash-and-carry"**: warring nations could buy non-military goods if they paid cash and carried them away in their own ships

Meanwhile aggression spread:
- **Japan** seized **Manchuria** (1931) and invaded **China** (1937).
- **Italy** conquered **Ethiopia** (1935).
- **Germany** under Hitler rearmed, took Austria, and took Czechoslovakia's Sudetenland after the **Munich Conference** (1938). There Britain and France practiced **appeasement**.

Roosevelt's **"Quarantine Speech"** (1937) suggested isolating aggressors but drew a hostile reaction.

## 2. From neutrality to "arsenal of democracy" (1939–1941)
Germany invaded **Poland** on **September 1, 1939**, and Britain and France declared war. Roosevelt took a series of steps toward the Allies:
- **1939:** Congress allowed **cash-and-carry for arms**, which favored Britain and France since they controlled the seas.
- **1940:** After **France fell** (June), Britain stood nearly alone. The **destroyers-for-bases deal** traded 50 old U.S. destroyers for leases on British bases. Congress passed the **first peacetime draft** (Selective Training and Service Act).
- **The debate:** The **America First Committee** (with **Charles Lindbergh**) argued for staying out. Interventionists such as the **Committee to Defend America by Aiding the Allies** answered them.
- **Election of 1940:** FDR won an unprecedented **third term** over Wendell Willkie. He pledged to make America the **"arsenal of democracy."**
- **January 1941:** The **Four Freedoms** speech named the goals: freedom of **speech** and of **worship**, freedom **from want** and **from fear**.
- **March 1941:** The **Lend-Lease Act** let the president lend or lease war supplies to any nation whose defense was vital to the U.S. It went first to Britain and later to the **Soviet Union**. This ended any pretense of neutrality.
- **August 1941:** FDR and Churchill issued the **Atlantic Charter**: no territorial gains, **self-determination**, free trade, and a future system of collective security. It previewed the United Nations.
- **Fall 1941:** U.S. warships escorted convoys and fought an **undeclared naval war** with German U-boats in the Atlantic.

In the Pacific, Japan moved into French Indochina. In July 1941 the U.S. **froze Japanese assets** and imposed an **oil embargo**. Negotiations failed. On **December 7, 1941**, Japan attacked the U.S. fleet at **Pearl Harbor**, Hawaii, killing about 2,400 Americans. Roosevelt called it "a date which will live in infamy." Congress declared war on Japan, and Germany and Italy declared war on the U.S. days later.

## 3. Fighting the war
The Allies chose a **"Europe first"** strategy, because Germany was considered the greater threat.

**Europe and North Africa:**
- **North Africa** (Operation Torch, late 1942), then **Sicily and Italy** (1943).
- The Soviets broke the German advance at **Stalingrad** (1942–43), at enormous cost.
- **D-Day** (**June 6, 1944**): **Dwight D. Eisenhower** commanded the largest amphibious invasion in history at **Normandy**, opening a western front.
- Germany's last offensive, the **Battle of the Bulge** (December 1944), failed.
- Germany surrendered on **V-E Day**, **May 8, 1945**. Allied troops liberating the camps revealed the full horror of the **Holocaust**, the Nazi murder of six million Jews and millions of others.

**The Pacific:**
- Early disasters included the fall of the Philippines and the **Bataan Death March** (1942).
- At **Midway** (**June 1942**), U.S. carriers sank four Japanese carriers. Midway was the **turning point** of the Pacific war.
- The U.S. then used **"island hopping"** (also called leapfrogging) under **Douglas MacArthur** and **Chester Nimitz**: seize key islands, bypass others, and move bomber bases closer to Japan. Major battles included **Guadalcanal**, **Iwo Jima**, and **Okinawa** (1945).
- The secret **Manhattan Project** (J. Robert Oppenheimer at Los Alamos) built the **atomic bomb**.
- **Harry Truman**, president after FDR's death in April 1945, ordered bombs dropped on **Hiroshima** (August 6) and **Nagasaki** (August 9). Japan surrendered (V-J Day, August 1945; formal surrender September 2). Truman argued the bombs saved the lives an invasion would have cost. Critics have debated the decision ever since, including whether Japan was already near surrender and whether the bombs were meant partly to impress the Soviets.

## 4. Wartime diplomacy
The "Big Three" were Roosevelt, **Churchill**, and **Stalin**.
- **Casablanca** (1943): Roosevelt and Churchill demanded the Axis's **unconditional surrender**.
- **Tehran** (1943): the Allies agreed on the **second front** in France.
- **Yalta** (**February 1945**) was the most controversial meeting:
  - Stalin agreed to enter the war against Japan and to join the **United Nations**.
  - He promised **free elections** in Eastern Europe, which he did not honor.
  - Germany would be divided into **occupation zones**.
  - Critics later charged that FDR "gave away" Eastern Europe, though the Red Army already occupied it.
- **Potsdam** (July 1945): Truman, Stalin, and Churchill (replaced midway by Attlee) set occupation policy and warned Japan to surrender.
- The **United Nations** charter was signed at **San Francisco** (1945). This time the Senate approved U.S. membership overwhelmingly.
- The **Bretton Woods** conference (1944) created the **International Monetary Fund** and **World Bank** and made the dollar the anchor of the world economy.

## 5. The home front
**Production won the war.** The **War Production Board** converted factories, and auto plants built tanks and bombers. The **Office of Price Administration** **rationed** goods (gasoline, sugar, meat, tires) and controlled prices. War bonds and a broader **income tax** (with paycheck **withholding**, starting in 1943) paid for it all. National output nearly doubled, and **unemployment virtually disappeared**, which ended the Depression.

**Women:** About **six million women** entered the workforce, symbolized by **"Rosie the Riveter."** Many did industrial work for the first time. Some 350,000 served in uniform (WACs, WAVES). Most were pushed out of industrial jobs when men returned, but the experience mattered for later change.

**African Americans:** The **Double V campaign** demanded victory over fascism abroad *and* racism at home. When **A. Philip Randolph** threatened a **March on Washington** (1941), FDR issued **Executive Order 8802**, banning discrimination in **defense industries** and creating the **Fair Employment Practices Committee**. Some 1.2 million Black Americans served in a **segregated** military, including the **Tuskegee Airmen**. A second wave of the Great Migration moved north and west to defense jobs. Racial tension exploded in the **Detroit riot** (1943).

**Mexican Americans:** The **Bracero Program** (1942) brought Mexican laborers for farm work. In the **Zoot Suit Riots** (Los Angeles, 1943), servicemen attacked young Mexican Americans.

**Native Americans:** About 25,000 served, including the **Navajo code talkers**, whose code the Japanese never broke.

**Japanese American internment:** After Pearl Harbor, **Executive Order 9066** (February 1942) led to the forced removal of about **120,000 people of Japanese ancestry** from the West Coast to **internment camps**. **About two-thirds were U.S. citizens.** No comparable mass action was taken against German or Italian Americans, and no Japanese American was convicted of espionage. In **Korematsu v. United States** (1944), the Supreme Court **upheld** the removal as a military necessity. Meanwhile the Japanese American **442nd Regimental Combat Team** became one of the most decorated units of the war. In **1988** Congress apologized and paid **$20,000** to each surviving internee.

**Refugees:** U.S. immigration policy kept most Jewish refugees out. The ship *St. Louis* was turned away in 1939. A **War Refugee Board** was created only in 1944.

**Looking ahead:** The **GI Bill** (1944) gave veterans money for **college, job training, and low-interest home loans**. It helped build the postwar middle class and suburbs (Unit 7). Defense spending pulled people and industry to the **West and South** (the future **Sun Belt**).
