## The big picture
The United States tried to stay neutral in the Great War, then joined it in 1917. It helped win, but the Senate **rejected the peace treaty**. At home the war brought propaganda, limits on dissent, and the **Great Migration**, then a postwar **Red Scare**. The 1920s were an age of **prosperity, mass culture, and modern values**. They were also an age of fierce **backlash**: immigration restriction, a reborn Ku Klux Klan, Prohibition, and the Scopes trial. The CLEP exam loves the **cultural conflicts of the 1920s**.

## 1. From neutrality to war (1914–1917)
When war broke out in Europe in 1914, **Woodrow Wilson** declared neutrality and asked Americans to be "impartial in thought as well as in action." But neutrality tilted toward the Allies:
- Americans shared language and ties with Britain.
- U.S. **trade and loans** to the Allies (Britain and France) far exceeded those to Germany, since Britain's naval **blockade** cut off German trade.
- Germany struck back with **submarine (U-boat) warfare**. In May 1915 a U-boat sank the British liner **Lusitania**, killing 128 Americans. After more sinkings, Germany promised in the **Sussex Pledge** (1916) not to sink merchant ships without warning.

Wilson won reelection in 1916 on the slogan **"He kept us out of war."** Then events in early 1917 pushed the U.S. in:
- In January, Germany resumed **unrestricted submarine warfare**.
- The **Zimmermann Telegram** was intercepted. In it, Germany proposed that **Mexico** join a war against the U.S. in exchange for recovering Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona.
- The **Russian Revolution** (March 1917) toppled the czar, so the Allies looked more democratic.

In April 1917 Congress declared war so the world might be **"made safe for democracy."**

!cec Unrestricted U-boat warfare + Zimmermann Telegram + economic ties to the Allies | U.S. declares war (April 1917) | Fresh American troops help the Allies win by November 1918

## 2. The home front
Total war meant an active federal government:
- The **Selective Service Act** (1917) drafted about 2.8 million men.
- The **War Industries Board** (Bernard Baruch) coordinated production.
- The **Food Administration** under **Herbert Hoover** relied on *voluntary* conservation ("meatless Mondays").
- **Liberty Bonds** financed the war.
- The **Committee on Public Information** (**George Creel**) produced propaganda to build support.

Civil liberties suffered. The **Espionage Act** (1917) and **Sedition Act** (1918) punished antiwar speech, and Socialist **Eugene Debs** went to prison for an antiwar speech. In **Schenck v. United States** (1919), Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes upheld these limits with the **"clear and present danger"** test. Anti-German hysteria followed: schools dropped German classes, and sauerkraut became "liberty cabbage."

The war opened opportunities. Factory jobs and the end of European immigration drew hundreds of thousands of Black southerners north in the **Great Migration** to Chicago, Detroit, New York, and other cities. Women took industrial jobs. Their war service helped win the **Nineteenth Amendment** (1920): **woman suffrage**. Moderate suffragists under **Carrie Chapman Catt** lobbied, while **Alice Paul's** National Woman's Party picketed the White House.

**General John J. Pershing's** American Expeditionary Force arrived in strength in 1918 and fought at Château-Thierry, Belleau Wood, and the **Meuse-Argonne**. The **armistice** came on **November 11, 1918**. About 116,000 Americans died, many from the **1918 influenza pandemic**, which killed roughly 675,000 Americans at home and in the service.

## 3. The fight over the peace
Wilson's **Fourteen Points** (January 1918) laid out his vision:
- open diplomacy and freedom of the seas
- free trade and arms reduction
- **national self-determination** for peoples of the old empires
- a **League of Nations** to keep the peace

At Paris, the other Allies wanted revenge. The **Treaty of Versailles** forced Germany to accept a **war guilt clause** and pay heavy **reparations**. Wilson accepted these compromises to get the League included.

In the Senate, **Henry Cabot Lodge** and the **"reservationists"** objected that **Article X** (collective security) could drag the U.S. into wars without Congress's approval. The **"irreconcilables"** opposed the treaty completely. Wilson refused to compromise, went on a speaking tour, and suffered a **stroke**. The Senate **rejected** the treaty, and the U.S. **never joined the League**.

!cec Wilson's idealism (Fourteen Points) vs. Allied demands and Senate fears about Article X | Senate rejects the Treaty of Versailles | U.S. stays out of the League of Nations; a weaker League; American turn toward "isolationism"

## 4. Postwar reaction (1919–1920s)
In 1919 a huge **strike wave** hit the country, including the **Seattle general strike** and the **Boston police strike**, which Governor Calvin Coolidge broke. Anarchist **bombings** followed. Fear of Bolshevism produced the **Red Scare**. Attorney General **A. Mitchell Palmer's** **Palmer Raids** (1919–1920) rounded up thousands of suspected radicals, many of them immigrants, and deported hundreds.

The **Sacco and Vanzetti** case showed the same mood. Two Italian immigrant anarchists were convicted of murder on weak evidence and executed in 1927.

Racial violence exploded in the **"Red Summer" of 1919**, with a deadly riot in Chicago, and in the **Tulsa Race Massacre** (1921), where a white mob destroyed the prosperous Black Greenwood district.

**Nativism** became law:
- The **Emergency Quota Act** (1921) set the first national-origins quotas.
- The **National Origins Act (Immigration Act of 1924)** set quotas at **2% of each nationality as of the 1890 census**. This deliberately favored northern and western Europeans, cut immigration from southern and eastern Europe, and **barred Japanese and other Asian immigrants**.

A **second Ku Klux Klan**, revived in 1915 and inspired by the film *The Birth of a Nation*, grew to millions of members by the mid-1920s. It was strong in the **Midwest** (Indiana) as well as the South. It targeted **Catholics, Jews, and immigrants** as well as African Americans, then collapsed amid scandal.

## 5. The politics of "normalcy"
**Warren G. Harding** won in 1920 promising a **"return to normalcy."** His administration was marred by the **Teapot Dome** scandal: Interior Secretary **Albert Fall** took bribes to lease naval oil reserves to private companies. **Calvin Coolidge** ("the chief business of the American people is business") and Treasury Secretary **Andrew Mellon** cut taxes on the wealthy, kept tariffs high, and favored business.

Farmers did not share the boom. Crop prices collapsed after the war. Coolidge vetoed the **McNary-Haugen** farm relief bills. In 1928 **Herbert Hoover** beat **Al Smith**, the first Catholic major-party nominee, partly because of anti-Catholic and anti-"wet" (anti-Prohibition) feeling.

Abroad, the U.S. avoided alliances but stayed active:
- The **Washington Naval Conference** (1921–22) produced the Five-Power Treaty, which limited battleship tonnage.
- The **Dawes Plan** (1924) used U.S. loans to help Germany pay reparations.
- The **Kellogg-Briand Pact** (1928) renounced war "as an instrument of national policy." It was idealistic and had no enforcement.

## 6. The new economy and mass culture
The 1920s economy boomed on **consumer goods**. **Henry Ford's** moving **assembly line** (1913) made the **Model T** affordable, and the **automobile** reshaped the country: suburbs, roads, motels, oil, rubber, and steel. **Electricity** spread, bringing radios, vacuum cleaners, and refrigerators. **Advertising** and **installment buying** (credit) encouraged people to spend. But income inequality widened, and many families bought on credit, weaknesses that mattered after 1929.

Mass culture created shared national experiences:
- **Radio**, starting with the first commercial station, KDKA, in 1920.
- **Movies**, including the first "talkie," *The Jazz Singer* (1927).
- **Spectator sports**, with heroes like Babe Ruth.
- **Charles Lindbergh's** solo flight across the Atlantic (1927).

The **"flapper"**, a young woman with bobbed hair and short skirts who smoked and danced, symbolized new freedoms. **Margaret Sanger** campaigned for birth control. **Alice Paul** proposed an **Equal Rights Amendment** in 1923.

## 7. Culture wars: the modern vs. the traditional
**Prohibition** (the **Eighteenth Amendment**, 1919, enforced by the **Volstead Act**) banned the manufacture and sale of alcohol. It cut drinking somewhat, but it bred **speakeasies**, **bootlegging**, and **organized crime** (Al Capone in Chicago). It was repealed by the **Twenty-first Amendment** in 1933.

In the **Scopes "Monkey" Trial** (1925), Tennessee teacher **John Scopes** was tried for teaching **evolution** in violation of state law. **Clarence Darrow** defended him, and **William Jennings Bryan** helped prosecute. Scopes was convicted (later overturned on a technicality). Urban newspapers, especially **H.L. Mencken's**, mocked the **fundamentalists**, and the trial became a symbol of the clash between rural religious tradition and urban modernism.

## 8. The Harlem Renaissance and the Lost Generation
The Great Migration made **Harlem** the capital of Black America and the center of the **Harlem Renaissance**:
- **Langston Hughes**, poetry and jazz rhythms ("The Negro Speaks of Rivers")
- **Zora Neale Hurston**, folklore and fiction
- **Claude McKay**, poetry ("If We Must Die")
- **Countee Cullen**, poetry
- **Alain Locke**, editor of *The New Negro* (1925)
- jazz and blues from **Louis Armstrong**, **Duke Ellington**, and **Bessie Smith**

**Marcus Garvey's** **Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA)** preached **Black pride**, economic independence, and a **"Back to Africa"** movement. It drew mass support before Garvey was convicted of mail fraud and deported.

White writers disillusioned by the war and by middle-class materialism formed the **"Lost Generation"**, a term from Gertrude Stein. Many lived abroad in Paris:
- **Ernest Hemingway**: *The Sun Also Rises*, *A Farewell to Arms*
- **F. Scott Fitzgerald**: *The Great Gatsby* (1925), a portrait of Jazz Age excess
- **Sinclair Lewis**: *Main Street* and *Babbitt*, satirizing small-town conformity and boosterism. He became the first American to win the Nobel Prize in Literature.
- **T.S. Eliot**: *The Waste Land*
