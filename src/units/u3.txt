## The big picture
Three linked stories run from about 1890 to 1914. **Farmers** revolted against railroads, banks, and tight money, which created the **Populist Party**. The U.S. then **went overseas**, taking an empire in the Caribbean and Pacific. And middle-class **Progressives** tried to fix the problems of industrial society through government, from city hall to the Constitution. Many Populist demands became Progressive laws.

## 1. The farmers' revolt and the Populists
After the Civil War, farmers faced **falling crop prices**, **high railroad rates**, and heavy **debt**. Prices kept falling because the money supply did not keep up with economic growth. That is **deflation**, and it made debts harder to repay. Farmers organized in stages:
- the **Grange** (1867), which won state railroad laws (see Unit 2)
- the **Farmers' Alliances** (1880s), including a separate **Colored Farmers' Alliance**, which created cooperatives
- the **People's (Populist) Party** in 1892

The Populists' **Omaha Platform** (1892) demanded:
- **free and unlimited coinage of silver** at 16 to 1, to inflate the currency
- a **graduated income tax**
- **government ownership of railroads and telegraphs**
- **direct election of U.S. senators**
- the secret ballot and an eight-hour day for government workers

Their candidate, James B. Weaver, won over a million votes. Speakers like **Mary Elizabeth Lease** ("raise less corn and more hell") energized the movement.

The **Panic of 1893** brought the worst depression yet. Unemployment soared, and **Coxey's Army** of jobless men marched on Washington (1894). The money question took over politics. **"Silverites"** wanted silver coinage to raise prices. **"Goldbugs"** (bankers, the Northeast) wanted the **gold standard**.

### The election of 1896
At the Democratic convention, **William Jennings Bryan** electrified delegates with his **"Cross of Gold"** speech: "You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." Democrats and Populists both nominated Bryan, a **fusion** that effectively ended the Populist Party as an independent force. Republican **William McKinley**, backed by **Mark Hanna's** massive corporate-funded campaign, won with the support of the industrial Northeast and Midwest and urban workers. The 1896 election was a **realigning election**: Republicans dominated national politics until 1932. The **Gold Standard Act** (1900) settled the money issue.

## 2. The new empire
Why did a nation born in anti-colonial revolution take colonies? The motives fall into four groups:
- **Markets:** business wanted outlets for surplus goods.
- **Sea power:** Captain **Alfred Thayer Mahan** (*The Influence of Sea Power upon History*, 1890) argued that great nations need a modern navy, coaling stations, and colonies.
- **Racial and religious mission:** Social Darwinism and Anglo-Saxon superiority (Josiah Strong) blended with missionary zeal.
- **The closed frontier:** with the frontier gone, some looked outward.

**Hawaii:** American sugar planters led the overthrow of **Queen Liliuokalani** in 1893. President Cleveland refused to annex the islands, but they were annexed in **1898** under McKinley.

### The Spanish-American War (1898)
Cubans revolted against Spain in 1895. Spanish General Weyler's brutal **"reconcentration" camps** were sensationalized by the **yellow press** of **William Randolph Hearst** and **Joseph Pulitzer**. In February 1898 the **de Lôme letter** insulted McKinley, and then the battleship **USS Maine** exploded in Havana harbor. "Remember the Maine!" became the rallying cry. Congress declared war, but the **Teller Amendment** promised the U.S. would **not annex Cuba**.

The war lasted about four months. **Commodore Dewey** destroyed the Spanish fleet at **Manila Bay** in the Philippines. **Theodore Roosevelt's Rough Riders** charged up San Juan (Kettle) Hill in Cuba. Secretary of State John Hay called it a **"splendid little war."** In the **Treaty of Paris** (1898), Spain gave the U.S. **Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines** (for $20 million), and Cuba became nominally independent.

The **Anti-Imperialist League**, whose members included Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, and Jane Addams, argued that colonies violated the principle of government by consent. Filipinos led by **Emilio Aguinaldo** had expected independence. The brutal **Philippine-American War** (1899–1902) cost thousands of American lives and far more Filipino lives.

Afterward the U.S. built controls over its new sphere:
- The **Platt Amendment** (1901) made Cuba a **protectorate**: the U.S. could intervene and got a naval base at **Guantánamo Bay**.
- In the **Insular Cases** (1901), the Supreme Court ruled that the Constitution does not automatically apply in full to the territories ("the Constitution does not follow the flag").
- The **Open Door Notes** (1899–1900) from **John Hay** asked the powers to keep **China** open to equal trade and to respect China's territorial integrity. The U.S. sent troops to help crush the anti-foreign **Boxer Rebellion** (1900).

### Roosevelt, Taft, and Wilson abroad
**Theodore Roosevelt**: "Speak softly and carry a **big stick**."
- **Panama Canal:** When Colombia balked at a treaty, the U.S. backed a **Panamanian revolution** (1903) and got the Canal Zone. The canal opened in **1914**.
- **Roosevelt Corollary** (1904) to the Monroe Doctrine: the U.S. would act as an **"international police power"** in Latin America to keep European creditors out, starting in the Dominican Republic.
- He mediated the end of the **Russo-Japanese War** (Treaty of Portsmouth, 1905) and won the **Nobel Peace Prize**.
- The **Gentlemen's Agreement** (1907): Japan limited emigration of laborers, and San Francisco reversed its segregation of Japanese schoolchildren.
- The **Great White Fleet** (1907–09) showed off U.S. naval power around the world.

**William Howard Taft**: **"Dollar Diplomacy"**, which meant using investment rather than force to gain influence, though Marines still landed in Nicaragua.

**Woodrow Wilson**: **"moral diplomacy"**, which in practice still meant intervention in Mexico, Haiti, and the Dominican Republic.

## 3. The Progressive Era (about 1900–1917)
Progressives were mostly **urban, middle-class, educated** reformers. They believed experts and an active government could fix the abuses of industrial capitalism, political corruption, and social disorder.

### The muckrakers
Roosevelt called investigative journalists **"muckrakers."** Their work built public pressure for reform:
- **Lincoln Steffens**, *The Shame of the Cities*: urban political corruption.
- **Ida Tarbell**, *The History of the Standard Oil Company*: Rockefeller's tactics.
- **Upton Sinclair**, *The Jungle* (1906): he aimed at workers' conditions in Chicago meatpacking, but the public reacted to the filthy meat. "I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach." The result was the **Meat Inspection Act** and the **Pure Food and Drug Act** (1906).
- Photographer **Lewis Hine** documented child labor.

### Making government more democratic
- The **secret (Australian) ballot**.
- The **direct primary**, letting voters rather than bosses pick nominees.
- The **initiative** (citizens propose laws), the **referendum** (citizens vote on laws), and the **recall** (remove officials).
- The **Seventeenth Amendment** (1913): **direct election of senators**.
- Wisconsin governor **Robert La Follette's "Wisconsin Idea"** used university experts in government.
- City commission and city-manager plans, first tried in Galveston, Texas, after the 1900 hurricane.

### Social justice
- The **Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire** (1911) killed **146** garment workers, mostly young immigrant women, behind locked doors. It led to new New York safety laws.
- In **Lochner v. New York** (1905) the Supreme Court struck down a limit on bakers' hours as a violation of "liberty of contract." But in **Muller v. Oregon** (1908), it upheld maximum hours for **women**, persuaded by Louis **Brandeis's** brief full of social data.
- Reformers like **Florence Kelley** fought child labor and won state laws.
- The **Anti-Saloon League** pushed **prohibition**, which became the Eighteenth Amendment (1919).
- Suffragists (NAWSA under **Carrie Chapman Catt**, and the more militant **Alice Paul**) pressed toward the Nineteenth Amendment (1920; see Unit 4).

**Race was Progressivism's blind spot.** Disfranchisement and segregation spread in the South, often defended as "reform." Wilson **segregated the federal civil service**. In response, **W.E.B. Du Bois** and others formed the **Niagara Movement** (1905) and then the interracial **NAACP** (1909), which fought through courts and publicity.

On the left, the **Industrial Workers of the World** (IWW or "Wobblies," 1905; Big Bill Haywood) sought one big union of all workers. **Eugene Debs's Socialist Party** won 6% of the vote in 1912.

### The Progressive presidents
**Theodore Roosevelt (1901–1909)**, the **"Square Deal"**:
- He was the **"trust buster"** who sued the **Northern Securities** railroad holding company (upheld by the Court in 1904). He separated "good" from "bad" trusts.
- In the **1902 anthracite coal strike** he forced arbitration. It was the first time a president intervened as a neutral party rather than for the owners.
- The **Hepburn Act** (1906) strengthened the ICC.
- **Conservation:** with **Gifford Pinchot** he set aside national forests, wildlife refuges, and monuments, and backed the Newlands Reclamation Act (1902). Pinchot favored wise *use* of resources (conservation). **John Muir** (Sierra Club) wanted *preservation* of untouched wilderness.

**William Howard Taft (1909–1913)**:
- He brought more antitrust suits than TR, including the 1911 breakup of **Standard Oil**.
- He angered progressives over the high **Payne-Aldrich Tariff** and the **Ballinger-Pinchot** dispute.
- The **Sixteenth Amendment** (income tax) passed Congress under Taft and was ratified in 1913.

**Election of 1912**: Roosevelt ran as the **Progressive ("Bull Moose")** candidate on **"New Nationalism"**, meaning accept big business but regulate it with strong federal power (inspired by Herbert Croly). Wilson's **"New Freedom"** aimed to break up trusts and restore competition. The Republican split elected **Woodrow Wilson**.

**Wilson's reforms (1913–1916)**:
- the **Underwood Tariff** (lower rates plus the first modern **income tax**)
- the **Federal Reserve Act** (1913), which created a central banking system to manage the money supply
- the **Clayton Antitrust Act** (1914), which strengthened the Sherman Act and **exempted labor unions**. Gompers called it labor's "Magna Carta."
- the **Federal Trade Commission** (1914)

!cec Muckrakers expose abuses; Populist ideas spread | Progressive reforms at city, state, and federal levels | 16th, 17th, 18th, 19th Amendments; regulatory agencies (FTC, Federal Reserve); expanded federal role

### Ideas and culture
- **Pragmatism** (**William James**, **John Dewey**) judged ideas by their practical results. Dewey promoted **progressive education**: "learning by doing."
- The **Ashcan School** painted gritty city scenes.
- The **Armory Show** (1913) introduced Americans to European modern art.
